## Select the editorial form

| Form | Best use | Natural progression |
|---|---|---|
| Perspective | Name and explain a business situation people feel but may misdiagnose | Situation → change → symptom → mechanism → alternatives → diagnostic → bounded perspective |
| Field note | Share a real observation or lesson from work | Context → what happened → decision → result → what changed → limitation |
| Method note | Teach how to inspect or solve a recurring problem | Problem → method → steps → why each matters → trade-offs → reusable tool |
| Evidence note | Answer a question through research or data | Question → method/sources → findings → contradictions → implication → limits |
| Case methodology | Explain work without overstating attribution | Context → constraint → choices → execution → observed outcome → attribution limits → lesson |
| Guide | Help a reader complete a concrete task | Goal → prerequisites → steps → examples → failure modes → verification |
| Founder note | State a personal, experience-backed view | Trigger → first-hand scene → interpretation → what changed → current belief → invitation |

Do not force every article into the same sequence. Retain only the sections needed to answer the reader’s questions.

## Develop a narrative seed

A short card is source material, not an outline. Expand it only by adding:

- present context or evidence;
- the plausible mechanism;
- competing explanations;
- observable signs;
- what the reader can inspect;
- what the proposed discipline can influence;
- what remains outside its control;
- a concrete next decision;
- sources and limitations.

Cut any paragraph that adds none of these.

## Extract first-hand material

When the author has relevant experience, interview for specifics before drafting:

- What exact moment made this problem visible?
- What did you initially believe was happening?
- What decision did you make, and what did you reject?
- What failed, surprised you, or changed your mind?
- Which details, numbers, artifacts, or messages still exist?
- What would a fair competitor or critic say?
- What remains uncertain?
- Which phrases are genuinely yours?

Encourage spoken or rough answers when that preserves the author’s natural language. Never convert a hypothetical answer into first-hand experience.

## Test the angle

Try only the lenses that fit the evidence:

- **Reframe**: the visible symptom belongs to a different underlying problem.
- **Tension**: two reasonable goals pull the business in opposite directions.
- **Hidden cost**: the obvious saving creates a less visible loss.
- **Leading indicator**: an earlier signal predicts the later commercial symptom.
- **Category error**: two different outcomes or metrics are being treated as the same.
- **Counter-case**: an exception reveals the boundary of the rule.

Choose the lens that produces the clearest defensible thesis, not the loudest title.

## Titles and openings

Prefer titles that state a tension, specific claim, story, or payoff. Avoid:

- vague topic labels;
- exaggerated “ultimate” or “definitive” claims;
- years added only for freshness theater;
- keyword-stuffed headings;
- clickbait that the article cannot repay.

Open with the situation or conclusion. Background belongs later, after the reader knows why it matters.

## Search without search-first writing

Use search research to understand the reader’s language, current questions, and existing coverage. Then:

- write for a known audience and need;
- add original analysis, evidence, or first-hand value;
- use descriptive titles and headings;
- cite authoritative sources;
- add a definition or FAQ only when the reader actually needs it;
- produce metadata that accurately describes the finished piece;
- include authorship, publication date, and update date where appropriate;
- add internal links based on conceptual relevance.

Do not optimize through keyword density, mechanical heading intervals, fixed word counts, or copying the average ranking structure.

## Closings

Choose one:

- a diagnostic the reader can run;
- a decision or trade-off to consider;
- a method, checklist, or source;
- an honest statement of what remains unknown;
- a restrained invitation connected to the article.

Do not restate the entire introduction or end with generic brand hype.

## Localization workflow

1. Lock the canonical thesis, facts, links, and qualifications.
2. Define the target reader and register in the destination language.
3. Rebuild sentences naturally while preserving meaning.
4. Localize title, dek, slug, excerpt, alt text, numbers, dates, and disclosure.
5. Compare the localized claim ledger with the canonical version.
6. Record material localization choices and request native review when risk is high.
